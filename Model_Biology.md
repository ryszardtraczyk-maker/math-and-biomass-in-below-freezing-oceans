Eco-Trophic Model: Icefishes in Sub-Ice Oceans (Antarctica vs Europa/Enceladus)
This document serves as supplementary reference material for the computer simulation contained within model.py and model.exe. It details the biological and physiological foundations of the systems of differential equations and periodical structures, and evaluates the evolutionary adaptation potential of these organisms within extraterrestrial ice worlds.

1. Introduction and Astrobiological Framework: The Europa and Enceladus Perspective
Imagine an ocean with no sunlight at all—no photosynthesis, no green plants, just permanent darkness, bitter cold, and powerful hydrodynamic currents, yet shielded from direct cosmic hazards by a thick ice shell—corresponding to the protective layer that shelters life on Earth. While currently a matter of science fiction that traces humanity's journey from the dawn of using rock flakes to our present interplanetary activity, this description closely reflects the physical reality of the hidden oceans beneath the icy crusts of Jupiter's moon Europa and Saturn's moon Enceladus. A fundamental question in astrobiology is whether such extreme environments, when shielded from outer Space, can support complex, multicellular animal life.
To explore this hypothesis, a mathematical model was developed for an ecosystem powered entirely by kinetic and chemosynthetic energy rather than solar radiation. At the base of the trophic food chain are chemosynthetic sulfur bacteria (Beggiatoa spp., with an individual mass of approximately 1 picogram = 1·10⁻¹² kg and a length of 10 µm) that utilize chemical gradients from deep-sea hydrothermal vents. The next trophic level consists of "biomass collectors"—sessile organisms analogous to cold-water corals (Lophelia pertusa / Desmophyllum) and deep-sea sponges. These organisms remain stationary, filtering particulate organic matter driven by powerful tidal currents. Thriving in total darkness, they are uniquely exceptional; unlike other corals living in the sunlit zones of the ocean, they do not require and do not possess symbiotic green algae to process solar energy. A single functional coral colony module is parameterized with a mass of 1000 g (1 kg), a height of 15 cm, and contains an average of 300 active polyps. At the apex of this trophic structure are apex predators inspired by Antarctic icefishes—unique vertebrates capable of thriving below the freezing point of seawater, possessing hemoglobin-free clear blood and extremely depressed metabolic rates.
The core characteristic of this ecosystem is the high stability of its biomass, which emerges from powerful, evolutionarily coupled interspecific dependencies. This vision of expanding the known boundaries of evolutionary life serves as a critical test for the ecological resilience of metabolic adaptations to permanent cryosphere conditions and non-solar trophic webs. It provides a theoretical framework to evaluate the survival potential of complex life on an interstellar scale, assessing its capacity to endure global catastrophic events (such as crustal shifts, continental drift, and massive glaciations) that have historically disrupted biosphere stability on Earth.
The morphology of sub-ice water cavities is highly complex. Unlike the smooth ice-water interfaces found in static terrestrial lakes, the continuous flow of turbulent currents and convective eddies carves an intricate, three-dimensional structure beneath the ice shelf, creating lace-like patterns and massive ice palaces analogous to frost patterns on windows. In this sub-ice labyrinth, the primary ecosystem engineer on Earth is the Antarctic krill (Euphausia superba Dana), which features an average total length TL = 50 mm and a body mass defined by the power law relation W[g] = a·TLᵇ (where a ≈ 0.0045, b ≈ 3.16). Its development is exceptionally well protected: eggs sink to a depth of 2 km, where larvae develop and grow at safe depths. Along with their development and the acquisition of a chitinous exoskeleton, they ascend to depth layers of 250–50 m. Under severe winter conditions, krill graze on the ice-algal biofilm up to 13 km inward from the ice edge, reaching extreme densities of up to 30,000 individuals/m³. In the absence of microalgae, krill switch to zooplankton predation (Drepanopus forcipatus, Oithona sp.). The collective biomass of krill, reaching billions of tons, actively modifies the local thermal and chemical properties of the water column, creating localized, oxygenated refugia that enhance fish larval survival. On the South Georgia shelf (surface area: 32,116 km²), krill do not successfully reproduce locally; instead, populations are continuously advected from the Antarctic Peninsula via the Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC), which is mathematically integrated into the model as a constant external advective flux.

2. Extraordinary Physiological and Anatomical Adaptations of Icefishes
Antarctic icefishes (Channichthyidae) represent the only known wild vertebrate radiation to have completely lost the hemoglobin gene cluster and functional erythrocytes. Their blood is entirely translucent, and oxygen transport occurs solely via physical dissolution within the blood plasma, a mechanism that operates at only 10% of the oxygen-carrying capacity found in red-blooded teleosts. Survival at −1.8 °C without respiratory pigments is driven by if profound anatomical and physiological compensations:
•	Cutaneous Respiratory System: Up to 90% of total metabolic oxygen requirements are met via transcutaneous diffusion. This process is mediated by an extensive network of monolayered, ultra-thin subepidermal capillaries embedded in the skin, fins, branchial cavities, and opercular membranes.
•	Low-Viscosity Hemodynamics: The total absence of red blood cells drastically reduces blood viscosity in sub-zero waters. According to the Hagen-Poiseuille law, plasma circulates with minimal vascular resistance. To maximize total oxygen flux, blood volume is expanded to approximately 9% of total body mass (2–4× higher than red-blooded teleosts), utilizing large-diameter vessels, expansive capillaries, and voluminous vascular sinuses.
•	Circulatory Energetic Cost: Driving this high-volume system requires significant cardiac energy expenditure. In red-blooded teleosts, cardiac output consumes roughly 5% of the total metabolic budget, whereas in hemoglobin-free icefishes, it demands up to 22% of total organismal energy.
•	Functional Role of Scaleless Skin: The complete evolutionary loss of scales does not serve to expose underlying capillaries (which anatomically reside above dermal structures anyway, as proven by Jakubowski), but rather functions to achieve ideal laminar fluid dynamics across the body surface. This limits drag under varying angles of attack in turbulent currents, accelerating boundary-layer flow, maximizing the local partial pressure gradient of oxygen across capillary walls, and minimizing turbulent noise around the mechanosensory lateral line system.
•	Skeletal Weight Reduction and Buoyancy: The absence of myoglobin in the axial skeletal musculature restricts high-frequency undulatory swimming, forcing a transition to energy-efficient pectoral fin propulsion (labriform swimming mode). Because Channichthyidae lack a functional swim bladder, neutral or near-neutral buoyancy is achieved via severe skeletal demineralization: the bony skeleton is extensively replaced by lightweight cartilaginous tissue, and ossification pathways are genetically suppressed. Consequently, the mass and density of the sagittal otoliths are greatly reduced, exhibiting lower aragonite crystal saturation and a highly porous microstructure.
Comparative Analysis of Three Key Species:
1.	Pseudochaenichthys georgianus (South Georgia Icefish, code SGI): A semipelagic species optimized for cryogenic conditions. The head comprises 44% of standard length (SL) and body depth represents 23% of SL, providing a vast surface-to-volume ratio for cutaneous gas exchange. Adults reach 55–60 cm TL with a mass of 1.5–1.6 kg. It uniquely retains myoglobin within its myocardium (despite a complete absence in skeletal muscle), which accelerates intracellular oxygen diffusion to cardiomyocytes by 600%. Its circulatory fluid is protected by the highest concentrations of antifreeze glycoproteins (AFGPs) in the family. Equipped with 3 distinct lateral lines, it detects eddies and current direction. It exhibits a distinct diel vertical migration pattern, resting on the benthos at 470 m during the day and migrating to pelagic zones at 200 m at night, navigating acoustically (as it lives in darkness) toward krill swarms concentrated within shelf eddies. Its sagittal otoliths are exceptionally tall (specialized for vertical acceleration detection), showing secondary elongation only during adult horizontal migrations along the continental slope. Populations exhibit geographic structure: specimens near the Palmer Archipelago (high krill larvae density) measure 16–34 cm, South Orkney populations consist exclusively of large adults (33–60 cm), and South Georgia hosts the full ontogenetic range (7–60 cm). Juvenile cohorts (age classes 1–2, 20–33 cm) aggregate in cold benthic currents on the southwestern shelf, while older adults occupy northeastern sectors. Spawn occurs in shallow coastal waters, and in continental Antarctic zones, spawning is delayed by 2–3 months relative to South Georgia due to thermal shifts. Total biomass exhibits a 4-year periodicity (cohorts rapidly gain mass in the 4th year, reaching ~1600 g) synchronized with multi-year climate cycles. Its diet consists of 68–70% krill.
2.	Chaenocephalus aceratus (Blackfin Icefish, borel): A strictly demersal (bottom-dwelling) predator possessing high tolerance for slightly warmer water mass intrusions. It reaches lengths of 75 cm TL and a mass of 2.1 kg (head 40% SL, body depth 20% SL). It exhibits the lowest mass-specific metabolic rate in the family (nearly half that of Ps. georgianus). It displays extreme axial muscle atrophy, skeletal demineralization, and the lowest aragonite saturation in its otoliths. The otoliths are massive and horizontally elongated, specialized for pitch-and-roll stabilization along the seafloor. It swims at a continuous velocity of 1.8 km/h. Energetically, it limits maintenance costs through a low surface-to-volume ratio (large body size) and a reduced density of thick-diameter white muscle fibers, limiting the active metabolic cost of maintaining electrochemical membrane potentials. Lacking myocardial myoglobin, it meets cardiac oxygen demands via a highly trabeculated, spongy ventricular lumen enlarged 3× relative to a trout heart of equivalent mass (320 g), absorbing oxygen directly from the luminal plasma pool. Larvae hatch in late winter/early spring in pelagic zones before recruiting to the benthos in older age groups. Adults feed on benthic organisms (55%) and krill (45%), avoiding direct competition with the South Georgia icefish and reaching peak biomass during warm-water anomalies when krill density drops, acting as an interspecific ecological buffer.
3.	Champsocephalus gunnari (Mackerel Icefish, code ANI, kergulena): A strictly pelagic, schooling species characterized by extreme hydrodynamic sleekness resembling salmonids (mean length 35–39 cm, mass at 49 cm equals 1029.5 g derived from the power equation W = 0.0017·TL³.⁴²¹). It is a faster swimmer than the aforementioned species and displays the highest mass-specific metabolic activity. It undergoes no axial muscle reduction; its locomotor muscles are densely vascularized with large-diameter capillaries and wide venous sinuses, minimizing internal oxygen diffusion distances. The otoliths uniquely retain a circular larval morphology throughout life, optimized as a balanced 3D angular acceleration detector for rapid banking and turns in open water. Cardiac oxygenation is sustained by massive stroke volumes (SV) and high cardiac output operating under sharp trans-branchial oxygen gradients. It exhibits the highest thermal tolerance in the family, driven by a specialized lipid composition in myocardial cell membranes and unique homeostatic pericardial peptides, coupled with the lowest AFGP expression (migrating north to the sub-Antarctic Kerguelen plateau). Larvae hatch latest (spring/early summer). Adults feed almost exclusively on krill (99%), forming dense, mobile schools in shallow pelagic layers, and total biomass routinely exceeds that of C. aceratus or Ps. georgianus by a factor of three. During prolonged open-water anomalies and ice-shelf retreats, it exhibits dwarfism (reducing adult size to 10–20 cm) and increases schooling density to survive prey crises.
The stable coexistence of these three species is maintained via comprehensive spatial-temporal niche segregation and sequentially staggered larval hatching seasons (winter – spring – summer). This strategy prevents simultaneous, concentrated predation pressure on the primary production base (ice algae and larval krill fleets), ensuring systemic trophic equilibrium.

3. Biomass Persistence and Periodical Mathematical Modeling
To quantify the non-linear dynamics of this sub-ice trophic web, a system of five coupled ordinary differential equations was formulated, incorporating Holling Type II functional feeding responses and lower-bound biomass constraints (CCAMLR protection thresholds) to prevent artificial numerical extinction. The state variables track the biomass densities of chemosynthetic bacteria (B), cold-water corals (F), the total normalized predator guild (P), ice algae (A), and Antarctic krill (K):
dB/dt = α · J_chem - β · B - k_BF · v(t) · B · F
dF/dt = η_BF · k_BF · v(t) · B · F - δ_F · F - γ_FP · F · P
dP/dt = η_FP · γ_FP · F · P - δ_P · P + 0.05 · g_PK · P · K
dA/dt = r_A · A · (1 - A / K_A) - g_K · K · A / (1 + h_A · A) - m_A · A
dK/dt = e_KA · g_K · K · A / (1 + h_A · A) - m_K · K - g_PK · P · K / (1 + h_K · K)
Where v(t) is a time-varying parameter for tidal current velocity and J_chem represents the chemosynthetic flux. The total predator biomass variable P is z-normalized and couples the three icefish species based on their relative biomass ratios on the South Georgia shelf: Ptotal = Pnorm · (0.45 + 0.50 + 1.50). A multi-year climate forcing function C(t) = sin(2πt / (365 · 4)) dynamically scales species dominance:
Pg(t) = (0.45 + 0.10 · C(t)) · P(t) [P. georgianus]
Ca(t) = (0.50 - 0.10 · C(t)) · P(t) [C. aceratus]
Cg(t) = 1.50 · (0.05 · K(t) / Kmax) · P(t) [C. gunnari]
This models the opposite-phase biomass oscillations of Pg and Ca, alongside the explicit tracking of Cg relative to total krill availability. Long-term biomass stability is achieved through a periodical formulation that suppresses numerical explosions, yielding stable, bounded limit cycles overlaid with an explicit trend function from historical records:
Biomasa(t) = exp(-β · t · 2⁻¹) · (A · sin[2π · (T⁻¹) · t + φ] + C)
Where A is the amplitude, T is the seasonal period (365 days), φ is the phase shift, C is the baseline mean biomass, and β is the decay/growth trend parameter governed by basal nutrient input.
The explicit kinetic modulation of metabolic rates and chemical fluxes by ambient temperature is governed by the Q₁₀ temperature coefficient relation:
P_chem(T) = P_chem(T₀) · Q₁₀^((T - T₀)/10)
Or through the physical Arrhenius formulation:
P_chem(T) = A · exp(-E_a / (R · T))
Where E_a is the activation energy, R is the universal gas constant, and T is the absolute temperature in Kelvins. Ectothermic vertebrate muscle tissue does not function as a heat engine—no Carnot cycle limitations apply since cross-bridge cycling is powered by ATP hydrolysis—meaning temperature acting as a key parameter modulates only enzymatic turnover kinetics. The total metabolic maintenance loss of the predator guild is defined as δ_P ∝ (P_chem - P_current) / E_biomass, where active muscle efficiency is bound between 25–30% (Eastman & DeVries 1982), propulsive swimming efficiency ranges from 15–25% (Blake 2004), and total net metabolic expenditure is minimized by 30–50% by actively harvesting environmental hydrodynamic current energy (Liao 2007).
Scaling Constants from Terrestrial Baseline (South Georgia Shelf)
Under heavy ice years (the baseline calibration state for the terrestrial model), parameters scale as follows:
•	Ps. georgianus: mean biomass 14,618 t (max 36,401 t, min 12,537 t) → 0.45 t/km² (Bmass = Bnorm * 0.45)
•	C. aceratus: mean biomass ~16,000 t → 0.50 t/km² (Bmass = Bnorm * 0.50)
•	C. gunnari: mean biomass ~48,000 t → 1.50 t/km² (Bmass = Bnorm * 1.50)
•	Krill (E. superba): mean biomass 1.3 · 10⁶ t → 40 t/km² (mean density 200 g/m², max 350, min 73; scales inversely with temperature: y = -0.25x + 2.24) → Bmass = Bnorm * 40
•	Ice Algae: mean biomass 1.2 · 10⁶ t → 37 t/km² (Bmass = Bnorm * 37)
•	L. pertusa (corals): mean biomass 5 · 10³ t → 0.16 t/km² (Bmass = Bnorm * 0.16)
•	Bacteria: mean biomass 1 · 10³ t → 0.03 t/km² (Bmass = Bnorm * 0.03)

4. Simulation Results and Evolutionary Potential: Earth vs Europa
Numerical integration of the eco-trophic equations reveals stark behavioral bifurcations when transitioning from the terrestrial baseline to the sub-ice oceans of Europa or Enceladus:
Earth Scenario (Open Coastal Shelf Model)
Total krill biomass never hits zero due to deep pelagic spawning and vertical advection down to 2–3 km. Under cold-water phases, Ps. georgianus dominates the upper tier, utilizing deep shelf eddies and dense sub-ice biofilms. Under modern warm-water anomalies, Ps. georgianus biomass drops sharply (current mean: 4,600 t, absolute minimum: 1,800 t), but system collapse is prevented by a compensatory rise in C. aceratus biomass, which switches to benthic predation on coral polyps. Concurrently, C. gunnari migrates to open surface waters to exploit pelagic krill fleets. Total combined fish biomass remains highly stable, and predation pressure is evenly distributed across time.
Europa / Enceladus Scenario (Permanently Capped Cryosphere)
Under a permanent ice shell (10–20 km thickness), all seasonal solar signaling and thermal ice-melt cycles disappear. The entire food web is driven strictly by hydro-thermal vent chemical plumes (J_chem) and convective tidal currents. All organisms are permanently confined within sub-ice water cavities. However, the high activity of ice shell movements provides pathways and fissures for migration. The restrictions on open-water migration (excluding krill development at 2–3 km depth) eliminates the un-coupling pathways that smooth out oscillations on Earth—meaning krill and predator guilds display significantly higher biomass volatility and extreme limit cycle amplitudes, making the system more prone to instability. However, because chemosynthetic bacterial production (B) is uninterrupted by winter light limitations and lacks summer thermal shocks, the system achieves stable long-term persistence. The simplified trophic web (few species, massive localized biomass) self-stabilizes without collapsing.
Hemoglobin-free icefishes present the highest initial theoretical survival and adaptation potential for extraterrestrial sub-ice oceans among all known terrestrial vertebrates (in Antarctica, fishes and other organisms inhabit depths of 2–3 km). While homeothermic mammals (humans) or birds require free gaseous atmospheres, stable surface pressures, and extreme caloric inputs to combat immediate thermal death in polar water, Channichthyidae are pre-adapted to the physics of Europa. The hyper-oxygenation of the extraterrestrial ocean (driven by surface ice radiolysis coupled with immense hydrostatic pressures) provides an ideal gradient for transcutaneous respiration (90% efficiency). The absence of hemoglobin eliminates metabolic dependencies on organic iron, which is highly scarce in lightless abyssal environments, and their cartilaginous skeletons readily withstand immense hydrostatic pressures. Furthermore, the capacity of Ps. georgianus to glide passively by capturing hydrodynamic energy (Liao 2007) allows it to easily navigate the water columns of sub-ice formations with near-zero oxygen consumption, making it an ideal vertebrate model for astrobiological ecosystem simulations.
